## Highlights of the Week

OpenAI found solutions to 10 open math problems and released them all at once.  
These were created by Astra(GPT6?), OpenAI’s next major model, which is said to be released soon.  
<https://openai.com/index/ten-advances-in-mathematics/>  
**Just grapple with the fact that consumer AI can now do the work of PhD mathematicians to some degree**. A German math professor, whose prior work was used for one of the problems, said he really liked the solution and it seemed more than just brute forcing. Instead, it seems that current AI is very good at combining existing approaches in novel ways and grinding away at solution candidates with low initial expectations of success, where humans would often give up. He does not expect really novel contributions that extend the realm of mathematics soon, like coming up with a new theory, but more of finding solutions within the convex hull of existing math knowledge.  
Here is a German interview with the math professor who contributed to one of the problems.  
<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RhqHJ6vLBek>  
 I guess in order to get to the next level, OpenAI hired one of the current winners of the Fields Medal, which is the highest prize in the math community.  
<https://www.theatlantic.com/technology/2026/07/jacob-tsimerman-math-fields-medal-openai/688120/>  
<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ulA7JNFFyYY>  
AI in mathematics is discussed quite controversially, as AI often uses prior work but doesn’t acknowledge it and was also trained, often illegally, on a lot of available math publications.  
Furthermore, research might be steered in a direction suitable for AI and not what really interests researchers. Additionally, publication of results through press releases or social media undermines and disturbs established scientific methods of peer review.  
‘**All of these challenges arise at a moment when the consequences of large-scale investment in artificial intelligence are being widely discussed in regard to warfare, mass surveillance, political disruption, and environmental damage. These raise grave ethical concerns. By failing to act, we run the risk of becoming complicit in the support of technologies which threaten much more than the practice of mathematics**.’  
<https://leidendeclaration.ai/>  
  
Here is a nice little blog post that argues that LLMs reward experience and competence, which I wholeheartedly agree with. So gain experience and competence in your field of interest so you can stand out among the slop.  
‘If you have no domain knowledge, you can cling onto the LLM to at least get something. That’s [not bad](https://www.seangoedecke.com/ai-makes-weak-engineers-less-harmful/)! But if you have domain knowledge, you can wring far more value out of the same LLM by steering it hard in the direction you want.’  
<https://www.seangoedecke.com/llms-reward-expertise/>  
  
Yuval Noah Harari did a brilliant interview where he points out that humans rely a lot on information-based systems like financial and legal systems, which they can barely grasp and where often one human cannot even grasp the entirety of a system. These domains will be perfect for AI (bureaucratic natives), which poses great challenges for humans as we will face superhuman bankers and lawyers that never sleep. Legally, humans and companies can be considered a person, but what happens once we have companies completely run by AI? If we grant them personhood, it is like ‘inviting the fox into the chicken coop’, with humans being the chickens. He also highlights that AI is not a mere tool, as it has agency, and that AI enables an extreme concentration of power.  
<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_V_ed5fuexA>

## Society, Safety and Miscellaneous

As AIs get more and more powerful and therefore more and more dangerous over 1.300 employees from frontier labs have signed a statement asking for pacing of the development so society can prepare for the coming changes (e.g. universal basic income) and also get time to solve safety issues.   
‘We request that the U.S. government support an international effort to develop the technical and governance tools needed to deliberately pace the frontier of automated AI development.’  
<https://www.pacingthefrontier.com/>  
Anthropic: ‘Our own research on recursive self-improvement, published last month, points to the need for tools to deliberately pace the frontier of AI development so society can prepare. We’re glad to see broad agreement across the field.’  
<https://x.com/AnthropicAI/status/2082228994653696371>  
Helen Toner, former OpenAI board member, says: There's a widespread idea in AI safety/governance circles that a Chernobyl-level disaster is the only way meaningful guardrails will ever be put on AI.’  
<https://x.com/hlntnr/status/2084357870674489697>  
<https://abcnews.com/video/135305423/>  
  
Cory Doctorow still doesn’t believe in the hype from the AI bros and thinks rather than replace humans, AI (especially open-source) will be useful for some people but useless and low impact for many, as the cost of running frontier models is much higher than the value of the generated output. The burst of the bubble will crash the economy, and then more reasonable narratives can take over. He also has a very interesting concept of a reverse centaur, where instead of AI helping humans, humans will become the bottlenecks in AI-driven workflows, leading to increased anxiety and lower quality of life. For example, AI will not be used to help the 10 radiologists in a hospital to give higher-quality diagnostics, but 9 radiologists will be fired, and 1 plus AI is now supposed to do the job of 10 and bears the sole responsibility for failures. Importantly, the interview does not touch much on the rise of AI capability, which is what interests me the most, especially if you see that you can pretty much run frontier models from last year now at home.  
<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rRRmUuxJolY>
